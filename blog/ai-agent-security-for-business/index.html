AI security for business

Ward Consulting AI · November 11, 2026

AI security for business starts with one question: what can the helper reach, and who decides that? This guide explains what a provider should do to protect your accounts and information, what you should never hand over to anyone, and how to check a provider's answers before you agree to anything.

Why security is a buying question

A helper that reads your inbox, drafts messages and looks at your calendar touches some of the most sensitive parts of your business. Security is not a feature you add later. It belongs in the first conversation, and a provider who treats it as an afterthought is showing you how the rest of the work will go. Ask before you connect anything, and keep asking as the work grows.

What a provider should do

  • Limit access to the information you choose, and say in writing what each helper can read.
  • Let you sign in to your own accounts yourself, so no one on the provider's side holds your passwords.
  • Start helpers in test mode, where they write drafts and do not send anything.
  • Require your yes before anything spends money, goes to someone for the first time or cannot be undone.
  • Give you a way to stop everything at once.
  • Explain in plain words who holds which account and how you would take it back.

Each item is a question you can test. Ask the provider to show you where the limit is set, who signs in where, and how the stop works. An answer you can see is worth more than an answer you are told.

What you should never hand over

  • Your passwords, or the answers to your security questions.
  • One-time codes sent to your phone or email.
  • Full card numbers, bank logins or account recovery codes.
  • Master access to every account at once.
  • Any secret key that lets someone act as you without asking you first.

If you have already shared one of these items, change the password or revoke the access right away, then tell the provider. A good provider will help you do that without asking for the secret itself.

A legitimate provider will not ask for these. If someone does, stop the conversation and check with the company through a number or address you already trust, not one the message gave you.

How access should be limited

Good access is narrow. A helper that sorts your inbox should see the inbox, not your bank. A helper that drafts a quote should see the scope and prices you entered, not your whole drive. When you add a new task, the new access should be a deliberate choice you make, and you should be able to remove it again without rebuilding anything. Narrow access also makes a problem easier to find. When a helper reads only one folder, a strange result points to one place to look. When it reads everything, the search takes much longer.

An example

For example, a contractor might let a helper read the quote requests that arrive by email and nothing else. The helper drafts a reply for each one and places it in a list. The contractor reads the list, sends the replies he approves and removes the access on the day he decides the helper is no longer needed. Nothing in that flow requires the helper to hold a password or a payment method.

Ask what happens when a helper's task ends. Access that stays open after a task is finished is the kind of gap that is easy to miss, so the provider should be able to show you how access is reduced when a task is retired. Keep a short written list of what each helper can read, and review it every few months.

Questions to ask a provider about security

  1. Which accounts will you need me to connect, and which of them can you not do without?
  2. Where do you write down what each helper is allowed to read?
  3. Will you ever ask for a password or a one-time code?
  4. Who can see my information on your side, and what happens to it if I stop?
  5. How do I pause everything, and how long does that take?

Keep a copy of the questions and the written answers you receive. If something changes later, you can point to what you were told rather than relying on memory.

Common questions

What should an AI provider do to keep my business data safe?

It should limit access to the information you choose, let you sign in to your own accounts, start in test mode, wait for your yes before spending or irreversible actions, and give you a way to stop everything at once. Ask for each answer in writing.

What should I never give an AI agent provider?

Never give your passwords, security answers, one-time codes, full card numbers, bank logins, recovery codes or master access to every account. A legitimate provider will not ask for them, and you should check any such request through a contact you already trust.

Can an AI helper read my whole email account?

It should only read what you point it at. Narrow access is the safer starting point, and any wider access should be a deliberate choice you can remove later. Ask the provider to show you exactly what each helper can read.
